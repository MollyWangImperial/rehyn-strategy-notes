# Special situations credit investing

`e5` · Event driven · Corporate credit

## Why the opportunity exists
### The Structural Opportunity: Forced Selling in Corporate Bonds

The investment case rests on corporate bonds whose prices fall below their estimated recovery value when refinancings, spin-offs and rating changes force holders to sell. The opportunity persists because many bond funds and insurers must follow rating and index rules regardless of price, while sanctions, trade restrictions and changes in monetary policy keep creating new stressed issuers.

The main risk is that a stressed issuer fails to recover, so the bond falls towards a low recovery value instead of rising towards the estimated one. Losses can also arrive together because forced selling spreads across many bonds in a market sell-off, as in December 2008 and March 2020. The estimated 9.4% annual return therefore comes with 8.8% volatility, an 18.0% worst fall and negative return skew, so a run of recoveries can hide the size of the loss in a stressed year.

## What Real Market Shocks Reveal About This Strategy

The large figure on each card shows the highest level of the US high yield credit spread during the episode, which is the extra yield investors demand over Treasuries to hold lower-rated corporate bonds. The spread has averaged about 5% over the long run, while readings above 8% signal severe stress in the corporate bond market.

**December 2008 · Financial crisis · 22% · strategy lost**  
Forced selling drives credit spreads to a record  
After Lehman Brothers failed, investors sold corporate bonds to meet redemptions, and the high yield spread reached a record 22% in December 2008. The strategy lost because bond prices fell far below recoverable value before markets stabilised.

**February 2016 · Oil · 9% · strategy gained**  
Oil price slump creates energy bond opportunities  
OPEC's decision to keep producing took oil below $30 a barrel and pushed the high yield spread to 9% in February 2016. The strategy gained because bonds of producers with strong balance sheets were priced for default, and spreads narrowed in the following months.

**December 2018 · Trade policy · 5% · strategy lost**  
Trade tensions and rate rises widen spreads  
US-China trade tensions and Federal Reserve rate rises led investors to sell high yield bonds in December 2018, lifting the spread to 5%. The strategy lost because forced sales by funds facing outflows pushed bond prices below estimated recovery values.

**March 2020 · Pandemic · 11% · strategy mixed**  
Fed support narrows spreads after a sharp widening  
The Covid sell-off lifted the high yield spread to 11% in March 2020, before the Federal Reserve announced support for corporate bond markets. Results were mixed because the strategy lost in the first weeks and then gained as forced sellers stopped and spreads narrowed.

**March 2023 · Banking · 5% · strategy mixed**  
Credit Suisse rescue writes off bank capital bonds  
Swiss authorities arranged UBS's takeover of Credit Suisse in March 2023 and wrote off about $17bn of its bank capital bonds, widening spreads on similar issues. Results were mixed because the write-off hurt holders of those bonds, while senior and secured credits recovered quickly.

**April 2025 · Trade policy · 5% · strategy gained**  
Tariff announcement briefly widens credit spreads  
The April 2025 US tariff announcement lifted the high yield spread to about 5% as investors reduced risk. The strategy gained because spreads narrowed within weeks, so bonds bought at the widest levels recovered.

### Today’s market watch

High yield spreads, the volume of bonds recently downgraded and the schedule of debt that matures in the next year are the main indicators because they show where forced selling is most likely. Rating agency decisions, sanctions and central bank policy matter most when they change borrowers' access to refinancing.

## Performance drivers and the geopolitical risks that matter most

**Favourable conditions**

- **Forced sellers leave the market**: The end of forced selling favours the strategy because bonds then return towards their recovery value. After the Federal Reserve announced support for corporate bond markets in March 2020, spreads narrowed sharply within weeks.
- **Refinancing markets stay open**: Open refinancing markets favour the strategy because stressed issuers can repay maturing debt instead of defaulting. After the oil price recovery in 2016, energy producers with sound balance sheets raised new funds and their bonds rose.
- **Rating rules create temporary mispricing**: Rules that force funds to sell downgraded bonds favour the strategy because the sales push prices below the value that issuers are likely to repay. In 2020, bonds of companies downgraded from investment grade to high yield recovered much of their loss within months.

**Adverse conditions**

- **Defaults exceed estimates**: A rise in defaults hurts the strategy because recovery values fall below estimates and bonds trade down towards them. In 2015 and 2016, many energy producers defaulted as oil prices fell below $30 a barrel.
- **Forced selling lasts longer than expected**: Prolonged forced selling hurts the strategy because bonds bought at a discount fall further before buyers return. After Lehman Brothers failed in 2008, the high yield spread rose to 22% in December and bonds fell below recovery estimates.
- **Liquidity disappears in a sell-off**: A loss of trading liquidity hurts the strategy because bid-ask spreads widen and positions cannot be reduced at fair prices. In March 2020, many corporate bonds could be sold only at large discounts until the Federal Reserve intervened.

## Where Else Can Investors Find Credit Premiums?

- **Direct lending** (`i7`): Lends directly to private companies at floating rates. Bears default and illiquidity risk, losing in recessions. *Steady income, with less price recovery*
- **Short-duration high yield investing** (`c6`): Holds short-dated high yield bonds for coupon income. Bears the risk that credit spreads widen in a sell-off. *Coupon income with short maturities*
- **Convertible bond arbitrage** (`r3`): Buys convertible bonds and hedges the share price. Bears the risk that credit spreads widen or liquidity falls. *Hedged credit exposure through equity*
