# Index futures mean reversion

`r7` · Mean reversion · Futures

## Why the opportunity exists
### The Structural Opportunity: Short-Term Index Overreaction

The investment case rests on the tendency of stock index futures to reverse part of a sharp one-day move that company earnings or economic data do not explain, because forced selling and large orders push prices past fair value. The reversal persists because index funds, leveraged investors and options hedgers trade at set times regardless of price, while policy announcements, tariff headlines and geopolitical news trigger moves that often fade as the information is absorbed.

The main risk is that a large fall marks the start of a sustained decline instead of a temporary overreaction, so buying after the fall adds to losses. This happened in October 2008 and March 2020, when falls continued for days or weeks as funding conditions worsened. Because the strategy earns small gains in most months, a single such episode can erase several months of returns.

## What Real Market Shocks Reveal About This Strategy

The large figure on each card shows the largest one-day fall in the S&P 500 during the episode. A one-day fall of more than 3% is uncommon, and it usually means that forced selling is driving prices.

**October 2008 · Financial crisis · −9.0% · strategy lost**  
Bank failures extend the sell-off for weeks  
The October 2008 sell-off followed the Lehman Brothers failure, and the S&P 500 fell 9.0% on 15 October as credit markets froze. The strategy lost because falls continued for days after each decline, so buying after a large fall added to losses.

**August 2011 · Sovereign downgrade · −6.7% · strategy gained**  
US credit downgrade triggers a sharp one-day fall  
The S&P 500 fell 6.7% on 8 August 2011, the first trading day after Standard and Poor's cut the US credit rating. The strategy gained because the index rose 4.7% the next day, so buying after the fall captured much of the rebound.

**June 2016 · Brexit vote · −3.6% · strategy gained**  
UK vote to leave the EU shocks markets  
The S&P 500 fell 3.6% on 24 June 2016 after the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union. The strategy gained because the index recovered most of the loss within a week as central banks signalled support.

**March 2020 · Pandemic · −12.0% · strategy lost**  
Covid lockdowns cause the largest fall since 1987  
The S&P 500 fell 12.0% on 16 March 2020, its largest one-day fall since 1987, as the pandemic forced lockdowns. The strategy lost because falls continued for another week, so buying each decline compounded losses.

**August 2024 · Carry trade · −3.0% · strategy gained**  
Bank of Japan rate rise unwinds yen trades  
The S&P 500 fell 3.0% on 5 August 2024 as a Bank of Japan rate rise forced the unwinding of yen-funded positions. The strategy gained because the index rose 1.0% the next day and recovered most of the loss within two weeks.

**April 2025 · Trade policy · −6.0% · strategy mixed**  
US tariffs cause a sharp fall and rebound  
The S&P 500 fell 6.0% on 4 April 2025 after the announcement of broad US tariffs, then rose 9.5% on 9 April when most were paused. Results were mixed because the index fell for two more days before the rebound began.

### Today’s market watch

The indicators that matter are the size of one-day index moves against their recent average, positioning in index futures and the cost of index options. Scheduled events such as central bank decisions, employment reports and tariff deadlines can turn a short-term reversal into a sustained move, so the strategy reduces its size around them.

## Performance drivers and the geopolitical risks that matter most

**Favourable conditions**

- **Falls caused by forced selling**: Falls caused by forced selling favour the strategy because the selling pressure ends once positions are closed, so prices recover. On 5 August 2024 the S&P 500 fell 3% as yen-funded positions were closed, and it recovered most of the loss within two weeks.
- **Clear central bank backstop**: Clear central bank support favours the strategy because investors buy falls when they expect policy to limit losses. After the Brexit vote in June 2016, central banks signalled support and the S&P 500 recovered most of its fall within about a week.
- **Range-bound markets**: Markets without a clear direction favour the strategy because rises and falls both reverse part of the way. The S&P 500 traded within a range from early 2015 to mid-2016, and short-term reversals were frequent.

**Adverse conditions**

- **Sustained sell-offs**: Sustained sell-offs hurt the strategy because each fall continues instead of reversing, so buying after a decline compounds losses. In October 2008 the S&P 500 had four daily falls of more than 5%, and in March 2020 it fell 12% in one day.
- **Policy shocks that reset valuations**: A policy change that alters expected earnings hurts the strategy because the new price level is justified rather than an overreaction. In April 2025, broad tariff announcements cut the S&P 500 by more than 10% in two days before a pause reversed part of the fall.
- **Surprises in inflation data**: Surprises in inflation or jobs data hurt the strategy because the move reflects new information, so it does not reverse. On 13 September 2022 the S&P 500 fell 4.3% after a higher inflation report and kept falling for most of the next month.

## Where Else Can Investors Find Short-Term Reversal Returns?

- **Cross-asset trend following** (`m1`): Follows sustained price trends across asset classes. Bears whipsaw risk, losing when trends reverse repeatedly. *Tends to gain when this strategy loses*
- **Equity pairs trading** (`r1`): Trades gaps between related stocks. Bears relationship risk, losing when a pair separates permanently. *Similar reversion, without index direction*
- **Variance risk premium harvesting** (`s1`): Sells index options priced for larger moves than occur. Bears crash risk, losing in rare large sell-offs. *Earns from the same overreaction in options*
