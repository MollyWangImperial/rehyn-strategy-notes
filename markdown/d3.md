# Safe haven currency allocation

`d3` · Defensive · FX

## Why the opportunity exists
### The Structural Opportunity: Safe Haven Currency Demand

The investment case rests on the tendency of the Swiss franc and the Japanese yen to strengthen when investors reduce risk, because both countries run current account surpluses and hold large net foreign assets. The pattern persists because residents bring foreign holdings home in a sell-off and because carry trades funded in yen and francs are closed, which creates demand for both currencies when geopolitical or policy tension increases.

The main risk is that these currencies can weaken for long periods when interest rates abroad are higher and appetite for risk is strong. Central banks can also lean against strength, as the Swiss National Bank did when it set a floor under EUR/CHF in 2011 and the G7 did when it sold yen in March 2011. The strategy therefore tends to lose steadily in calm markets, because holding low yielding currencies costs the interest rate gap, and the protection it provides is uneven.

## What Real Market Shocks Reveal About This Strategy

The large figure on each card shows the percentage change in USD/JPY, the number of Japanese yen needed to buy one US dollar, over the episode. A negative figure means the yen strengthened against the dollar, which benefits holders of yen.

**September 2008 · Financial crisis · −17% · strategy gained**  
Financial crisis forces investors to buy yen  
The unwinding of yen-funded positions after the Lehman Brothers failure in September 2008 pushed USD/JPY down about 17 per cent by December. The strategy gained because the yen strengthened as banks and investors repaid yen loans.

**March 2011 · Natural disaster · −6% · strategy mixed**  
Japan earthquake lifts the yen to a record  
The March 2011 earthquake in Japan pushed USD/JPY down about 6 per cent to a record low near 76 as insurers were expected to repatriate funds. Results were mixed because the G7 sold yen on 18 March and reversed most of the gain.

**December 2012 · Bank of Japan · +30% · strategy lost**  
Bank of Japan easing weakens the yen sharply  
Aggressive monetary easing from the new government and the Bank of Japan lifted USD/JPY about 30 per cent between November 2012 and May 2013. The strategy lost because the yen weakened sharply, and haven currencies gave no protection in a rising equity market.

**June 2016 · Brexit · −7% · strategy gained**  
Brexit vote sends investors into the yen  
The June 2016 vote to leave the European Union drove investors into the yen, and USD/JPY fell about 7 per cent in a day. The strategy gained because both the yen and the Swiss franc strengthened as investors sought safety.

**March 2022 · Rate gap · +32% · strategy lost**  
Rate gap weakens the yen despite war  
From January to October 2022, USD/JPY rose about 32 per cent as the Fed raised rates while the Bank of Japan capped bond yields. The strategy lost because the yen weakened despite the war in Ukraine, as the rate gap outweighed demand for safety.

**August 2024 · Carry trade · −12% · strategy gained**  
Yen carry trades unwind after a rate rise  
A Bank of Japan rate rise and weak US jobs data in July 2024 drove USD/JPY down about 12 per cent in five weeks. The strategy gained because the yen strengthened as investors repaid yen loans used for carry trades.

The gap between US interest rates and Japanese or Swiss rates, implied volatility on yen and franc options and the VIX are the main indicators. Central bank meetings in the US, Japan and Switzerland matter because a change in guidance can alter both the cost of holding these currencies and the risk of intervention. ## Performance drivers and the geopolitical risks that matter most

**Favourable conditions**

- **Equity sell-offs and carry unwinds**: Falling equity markets favour the strategy because investors close yen and franc funded positions and buy the currencies back. In August 2024, a Bank of Japan rate rise and weak US jobs data pushed USD/JPY down about 12 per cent in five weeks.
- **Falling US interest rates**: A narrower interest rate gap favours the strategy because it lowers the cost of holding yen and francs and reduces the incentive to borrow them. In 2008 the Fed cut its policy rate to near zero, and USD/JPY fell about 17 per cent between September and December.
- **Political shocks in Europe**: Political and financial stress in Europe favours the Swiss franc because investors move funds out of the euro area into Swiss assets. During the euro area debt crisis in 2011, EUR/CHF fell from above 1.25 to near 1.01 before the Swiss National Bank set a floor.

**Adverse conditions**

- **A wide interest rate gap**: A wide gap between US and Japanese rates hurts the strategy because holders of yen pay the difference through forward points while borrowers sell it. In 2022, USD/JPY rose about 32 per cent as the Fed raised rates and the Bank of Japan kept yields low.
- **Central bank intervention**: Official action against a strong currency hurts the strategy because it can reverse gains within days. In March 2011 the G7 sold yen after the earthquake, and in September 2011 the Swiss National Bank set a floor under EUR/CHF at 1.20.
- **Strong risk appetite**: Rising equity markets hurt the strategy because carry trades are rebuilt using the lowest yielding currencies. Between November 2012 and May 2013, USD/JPY rose about 30 per cent while the S&P 500 gained more than 15 per cent.

## Where Else Can Investors Find Safe Haven Returns?

- **G10 FX carry trade** (`c1`): Borrows low yielding currencies to buy high yielding ones. Bears crash risk, losing when carry trades unwind. *Earns carry from the same currencies*
- **Strategic gold allocation** (`d2`): Holds gold as a store of value. Bears real yield risk, losing when yields rise. *Safe haven through metal instead of currencies*
- **FX trend following** (`m4`): Follows exchange rate trends in both directions. Bears reversal risk, losing when trends turn abruptly. *Can hold these currencies when they trend*
