# Dividend quality investing

`i4` · Income · Equities

## Why the opportunity exists
### The Structural Opportunity: Reliable Dividend Income

The investment case rests on companies that pay dividends well covered by free cash flow, because those payments are cut less often and tend to grow with earnings. The opportunity persists because many investors chase headline yield without checking cash flow, which leaves companies with covered and growing payouts priced like the average company, while tax changes, bank payout limits and tariffs keep altering which payouts are safe.

The main risk is a fall in share prices larger than the income collected, because dividend shares still carry the full risk of the equity market. Dividends are cut in recessions when profits fall, as in March 2020 when many companies suspended payments to preserve cash. Higher bond yields also reduce the appeal of dividend income, so rate-sensitive holdings such as utilities can fall even when earnings are stable.

## What Real Market Shocks Reveal About This Strategy

The large figure on each card shows the change in the S&P 500 over the episode, measured from its high before the event to its low, or from its low to its high in a rally. It is a simple measure of how severe the move in the equity market was, so readers can compare how a dividend portfolio behaved against it.

**August 2011 · Credit rating · −19% · strategy mixed**  
US downgrade drives a defensive rotation  
The S&P 500 fell about 19% from April to October 2011 after the US credit rating downgrade and the euro area debt crisis. Results were mixed because covered dividends held up and drew defensive buying, but share prices still fell.

**May 2013 · Fed guidance · −6% · strategy lost**  
Taper signal lifts yields and hits dividend shares  
The Federal Reserve signalled in May 2013 that it would slow bond purchases, and the S&P 500 fell about 6% in the following month. The strategy lost because higher bond yields made dividend income less attractive, especially for utilities.

**December 2018 · Tighter policy · −20% · strategy mixed**  
Tighter policy and trade tension weigh on equities  
The S&P 500 fell about 20% from September to December 2018 as the Federal Reserve raised rates and US-China tariffs weighed on growth. Results were mixed because dividend payers fell less than the index but still lost value.

**March 2020 · Pandemic · −34% · strategy lost**  
Covid lockdowns force dividend cuts  
The S&P 500 fell about 34% in five weeks to 23 March 2020, and many companies cut dividends to preserve cash. The strategy lost because dividend cuts reduced income and share prices fell with the market.

**November 2020 · Vaccine rotation · +11% · strategy gained**  
Vaccine news revives cyclical dividend shares  
The S&P 500 rose about 11% in November 2020 after the first effective Covid vaccine results, and investors moved into energy, banks and industrial companies. The strategy gained because dividend payers in these sectors recovered as earnings expectations improved.

**April 2025 · Trade policy · −19% · strategy mixed**  
US tariffs trigger a sharp equity sell-off  
The S&P 500 fell about 19% from February to 8 April 2025 as sweeping US tariffs raised the risk of weaker earnings. Results were mixed because dividend payers fell less than the index, but companies with imported inputs faced margin pressure.

- Entry: Free cash flow covers the dividend at least 1.5 times
- Position size: No stock above 4% and no sector above 20%
- Exit: Position closed after a dividend cut, or when cover falls below 1.2 times
- Stop-loss: Equity weight cut by a third if the S&P 500 falls 20% from its high

### Today’s market watch

Rehyn monitors dividend cover, net debt against earnings and the gap between dividend yields and government bond yields, because these show whether payouts are safe and attractive. Corporate earnings seasons, central bank rate decisions and changes to dividend tax or bank payout rules are the events that matter most. A rise in bond yields would weigh on rate-sensitive payers, while weak earnings would put the weakest payouts at risk.

## Performance drivers and the geopolitical risks that matter most

**Favourable conditions**

- **Defensive rotations in sell-offs**: Defensive rotations favour the strategy because investors move into companies with steady cash flow and covered dividends when growth expectations fall. In 2022 the S&P 500 fell about 18% while dividend-focused funds lost far less, because earnings visible today were valued above growth expected years ahead.
- **Rising corporate cash flow**: Rising free cash flow favours the strategy because companies can raise dividends without borrowing, which lifts both income and share prices. From 2010 to 2019 S&P 500 dividends per share more than doubled as earnings grew.
- **Stable or falling bond yields**: Stable bond yields favour the strategy because dividend income stays attractive against government bonds, so valuations of dividend payers hold. In 2019 the 10-year Treasury yield fell from about 2.7% to 1.9%, and dividend payers gained alongside the market.

**Adverse conditions**

- **Recessions that force dividend cuts**: Recessions hurt the strategy because falling profits make companies cut payouts, which lowers income and weakens share prices together. In March 2020 companies in energy, banking and airlines suspended or cut dividends, which caused large losses for portfolios that held them.
- **Rising bond yields**: Rising bond yields hurt the strategy because investors can earn similar income from government bonds, which lowers the valuation of dividend shares. In May and June 2013 the 10-year Treasury yield rose by nearly 1 percentage point and US utilities fell about 10%.
- **Tax and regulatory limits on payouts**: Regulatory limits hurt the strategy when authorities restrict dividends to protect capital in a crisis. In 2020 European and British regulators asked banks to suspend dividends, which removed a large share of income from regional dividend benchmarks.

## Where Else Can Investors Find Equity Income?

- **Covered call writing** (`i5`): Sells call options on shares for premium. Bears capped gains, giving up most of a strong rally. *Adds option premium to share income*
- **Real asset income investing** (`i6`): Collects contracted rent and regulated cash flows. Bears real interest rate risk, with asset values falling when yields rise. *Property and utility income, not dividends*
- **Minimum volatility equity** (`d5`): Holds the least volatile shares. Bears the risk of lagging in strong rallies, with smaller drawdowns. *Defensive equity without an income focus*
