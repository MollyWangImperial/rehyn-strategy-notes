# Value factor investing

`v3` · Value · ETFs and funds

## Why the opportunity exists
### The Structural Opportunity: Compensation for Investor Extrapolation

The investment case rests on the tendency of investors to extrapolate strong growth too far into the future and neglect companies with weak recent results, leaving cheaper shares priced for outcomes that are easier to exceed. The premium persists because career risk discourages managers from holding unpopular companies, while regulation, industrial policy and geopolitical change repeatedly shift earnings towards previously discounted sectors.

The main risk is that cheap companies remain cheap because their business models, balance sheets or industries are deteriorating rather than temporarily out of favour. Value can also underperform for many years when falling interest rates and rapid technological change support long-duration growth shares, so investors may leave before the premium recovers. Negative return skew compounds this timing risk because recessions often expose financial leverage and weak margins across value-heavy sectors at the same time.

## What Real Market Shocks Reveal About This Strategy

The large figure on each card shows the calendar-year return of the MSCI World Value Index relative to the MSCI World Growth Index, rounded to the nearest percentage point. A positive figure means value outperformed growth, while a negative figure means the value tilt detracted from relative returns.

**December 2008 · Financial crisis · −7pp · strategy lost**  
Bank losses overwhelm cheap shares  
Value shares underperformed as bank losses, funding stress and collapsing commodity demand concentrated damage in already cheap sectors. The strategy lost because low valuations did not compensate for falling earnings and forced capital raising.

**December 2016 · Fiscal policy · +4pp · strategy gained**  
Fiscal reflation revives value shares  
Value outperformed after the US election raised expectations for tax cuts, infrastructure spending and lighter financial regulation. The strategy gained because bond yields rose and investors upgraded earnings expectations for banks, energy producers and industrial companies.

**December 2018 · Trade policy · −6pp · strategy lost**  
Trade conflict favours defensive growth  
Value underperformed as US-China tariffs weakened manufacturing activity and raised concern about global demand. The strategy lost because industrial, financial and commodity shares faced falling earnings estimates, while large technology companies retained stronger growth.

**December 2020 · Pandemic · −34pp · strategy lost**  
Pandemic policy widens the growth premium  
Value recorded exceptional relative losses as lockdowns damaged banks, energy producers and consumer-facing companies. The strategy underperformed because monetary stimulus lowered discount rates while remote working accelerated earnings growth for technology and internet businesses.

**December 2022 · War · +21pp · strategy gained**  
Inflation and war reverse growth leadership  
Value strongly outperformed as Russia’s invasion of Ukraine lifted energy prices and central banks raised rates to contain inflation. The strategy gained because energy and financial earnings improved while higher discount rates reduced the value of distant growth profits.

**December 2023 · Technology · −18pp · strategy lost**  
Artificial intelligence restores growth leadership  
Value underperformed as enthusiasm for artificial intelligence concentrated market gains in a small group of expensive technology companies. The strategy lost relative ground because earnings upgrades and rising index weights outweighed concerns about high valuations.

### Today’s market watch

Value spreads, earnings revisions and market concentration indicate whether cheap shares offer compensation for weaker fundamentals. Central bank decisions, fiscal policy, trade restrictions and energy supply changes matter because they can shift rates, margins and sector leadership. Major technology earnings also matter because further index concentration would favour growth over value.

## Performance drivers and the geopolitical risks that matter most

**Favourable conditions**

- **Rates rise with nominal growth**: Higher rates favour value when they reflect stronger nominal activity because bank margins, commodity revenues and industrial earnings tend to improve. This combination supported value after the 2016 US election and during much of 2022.
- **Market leadership broadens**: Broader earnings growth favours value because returns become less dependent on a small group of highly valued companies. The vaccine announcements in November 2020 began such a rotation as banks, travel companies and manufacturers recovered.
- **Industrial policy lifts investment**: Public investment favours value when subsidies and infrastructure spending raise order backlogs for manufacturers, materials companies and utilities. US and European energy-security programmes after Russia’s 2022 invasion supported capital spending across several value-heavy sectors.

**Adverse conditions**

- **Falling rates favour growth**: Lower discount rates hurt value when investors pay more for distant earnings and favour companies with limited near-term cash flow. This mechanism contributed to value’s prolonged underperformance during the low-rate period before 2021.
- **Cheap sectors face disruption**: Technological disruption hurts value when low multiples reflect a lasting decline in demand, margins or market share. Traditional retailers and media companies provided repeated examples during the 2010s as online platforms captured revenue.
- **Recessions expose weak balance sheets**: Recessions hurt value when leveraged companies face falling revenue, tighter credit and pressure to issue equity. Financial and energy shares suffered this combination during March 2020, producing the strategy’s deepest drawdown in the test period.

## Where Else Can Investors Find Equity Factor Returns?

- **Equity momentum investing** (`m2`): Follows relative price strength rather than cheapness. It bears reversal risk after crowded trends. *Complements value across leadership cycles*
- **Dividend quality investing** (`i4`): Emphasises durable dividends rather than low multiples. It bears equity and payout-cut risk. *Adds income and balance-sheet discipline*
- **Minimum volatility equity** (`d5`): Selects lower-risk shares rather than cheaper shares. It bears valuation risk when defensive stocks become expensive. *Reduces exposure to value drawdowns*
